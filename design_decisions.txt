HEALTHCARE ANALYTICS LAB - PART 3.1: STAR SCHEMA DESIGN DECISIONS
=================================================================

DECISION 1: FACT TABLE GRAIN
----------------------------
Choice: OPTION A - one row per encounter (fact_encounters).

Why this is best for the four questions:

- Q1 (encounters and unique patients by month, specialty, type) counts
  ENCOUNTERS. At encounter grain, COUNT(*) is the answer. At diagnosis grain
  (Option B) or procedure grain (Option C) every encounter is repeated 1-4
  times, so every count would need COUNT(DISTINCT encounter) to undo the
  duplication.

- Q4 (revenue) sums billing per encounter. At Option B or C grain the billed
  amount would be repeated on each diagnosis or procedure row and SUM() would
  overstate revenue unless the dollars were split or allocated, which is
  error-prone. At encounter grain each dollar appears exactly once.

- Q3 (readmissions) is an encounter-to-encounter relationship. One row per
  stay lets the ETL store a single readmitted_within_30d flag, and the query
  becomes SUM(flag) / COUNT(*).

- Q2 (diagnosis-procedure pairs) is the only question that needs detail below
  the encounter. Option B would serve diagnoses but not procedures, and Option
  C the reverse; neither covers both. Bridge tables hang both lists off the
  encounter-grain fact without changing its grain (Decision 4).

- Size: 1,000,000 encounter rows versus 2,500,000 rows (B) or 2,000,000 rows
  (C), with no information lost.

Encounter grain is the "natural" business event: an admission or visit is
what operations, finance and quality all count.


DECISION 2: DIMENSION TABLES
----------------------------
All dimensions use an integer SURROGATE key. The OLTP id is kept as the
natural key for lookups. Key 0 in every dimension is an "Unknown" member, so
fact foreign keys are never NULL and inner joins never silently drop rows.

dim_date (static, one row per day 2015-01-01 .. 2030-12-31 = 5,844 rows)
  date_key (YYYYMMDD smart key, e.g. 20240510), calendar_date, day_of_month,
  day_of_week, day_name, is_weekend, week_of_year, month_num, month_name,
  month_label ('2024-05'), month_key (202405), quarter_num, year_quarter
  ('2024-Q2'), year_num.
  Why: months, quarters and weekends are pre-computed once instead of
  DATE_FORMAT() on every row of every query. The YYYYMMDD key is readable, and
  date_key DIV 100 gives the month directly.
  Role-playing: the fact references it twice (encounter_date_key,
  discharge_date_key), and the procedure bridge once (procedure_date_key).

dim_patient (SCD Type 2)
  patient_key, patient_id (natural key), mrn, first_name, last_name,
  full_name, date_of_birth, birth_year, gender, gender_desc, age_group
  ('0-17','18-34','35-49','50-64','65-79','80+'), effective_from,
  effective_to, is_current, row_hash.
  Note: age_group is the CURRENT age band, useful for "who are our patients
  today". Age at the time of care changes with every visit, so it is stored on
  the fact (patient_age_at_encounter).

dim_provider (SCD Type 2)
  provider_key, provider_id, first_name, last_name, full_name, credential,
  specialty_key, specialty_name, specialty_code, department_key,
  department_name, effective_from, effective_to, is_current, row_hash.
  Specialty and department names are DENORMALIZED onto the provider, so a
  provider report never needs a snowflake chain. SCD2 matters here: if Dr.
  Chen moves from Cardiology to Internal Medicine, his past encounters must
  stay credited to Cardiology. This was tested and works.

dim_specialty (SCD Type 1)
  specialty_key, specialty_id, specialty_name, specialty_code.

dim_department (SCD Type 1)
  department_key, department_id, department_name, floor, capacity.

dim_encounter_type (static, 4 rows)
  encounter_type_key, type_name ('Outpatient','Inpatient','ER','Unknown'),
  care_setting ('Ambulatory','Acute','Emergency'), is_inpatient flag.
  Why a dimension for 3 values: filters become integer comparisons, and the
  is_inpatient flag carries business meaning (Q3 asks for "is_inpatient = 1",
  not a hard-coded string).

Others - yes:
dim_diagnosis (SCD Type 1)
  diagnosis_key, diagnosis_id, icd10_code, icd10_description,
  icd10_category (first 3 characters, e.g. 'I50'), icd10_chapter (first
  letter, e.g. 'I' = circulatory). The category and chapter allow roll-ups
  that the OLTP cannot do without string functions.
dim_procedure (SCD Type 1)
  procedure_key, procedure_id, cpt_code, cpt_description.


DECISION 3: PRE-AGGREGATED METRICS ON THE FACT
----------------------------------------------
Stored on every fact_encounters row, computed once by the ETL:

  diagnosis_count          COUNT of encounter_diagnoses rows
  procedure_count          COUNT of encounter_procedures rows
  claim_count              COUNT of billing rows
  total_claim_amount       SUM(billing.claim_amount)
  total_allowed_amount     SUM(billing.allowed_amount)
  length_of_stay_hours     discharge - admission, in hours
  length_of_stay_days      calendar days (inpatient LOS)
  patient_age_at_encounter age on the encounter date
  is_inpatient             flag
  readmitted_within_30d    1 if THIS inpatient stay was followed by another
                           inpatient admission within 30 days of discharge
  is_readmission           1 if THIS stay is itself a readmission
  days_to_next_admission   days from discharge to the next admission
  primary_diagnosis_key    the diagnosis with sequence = 1 (a dimension key,
                           kept for convenience)

Also: specialty_key is stored DIRECTLY on the fact, copied from the
provider's specialty version at the time of the encounter. It is not a metric,
but it is the single most important denormalization. Every one of the four
questions groups by specialty, and this removes the encounter -> provider ->
specialty hop that dominated all four OLTP plans.

Why pre-aggregating helps:
- It turns query-time JOIN + GROUP BY into a column read. Q4 in the OLTP
  joins 950k billing rows to encounters and aggregates; in the star schema the
  sum per encounter already exists, so Q4 reads one integer-keyed index.
- It moves expensive logic to the ETL, where it runs once. The readmission
  self-join is the extreme case: it cost 13.6 s per OLTP query on MySQL. In the star
  schema it runs once per load and Q3 becomes SUM(readmitted_within_30d), now
  0.22 s (63x faster).
- It makes metrics consistent. Every analyst gets the same readmission
  definition and the same LOS calculation, instead of each rewriting it
  slightly differently.
- It enables covering indexes. Because the measures live on the fact row, they
  can be INCLUDED in an index, and queries never touch the base table (see
  star_schema.sql).
- Additivity: all stored amounts and counts are additive, so they can be
  summed safely at any level (month, specialty, department). Flags are
  0/1, so SUM(flag)/COUNT(*) gives a correct rate at any level.

What I deliberately did NOT pre-aggregate on the fact: unique patient counts.
COUNT(DISTINCT) is not additive; daily distinct counts cannot be summed into
monthly distinct counts. Q1 computes it at query time from patient_key.


DECISION 4: BRIDGE TABLES
-------------------------
Choice: YES - bridge_encounter_diagnoses and bridge_encounter_procedures, plus
an optional aggregate table, agg_diagnosis_procedure_pairs.

Why bridges:
- Encounters to diagnoses and encounters to procedures are genuinely
  many-to-many (1-4 diagnoses, 1-3 procedures per encounter). Putting them
  into the encounter-grain fact would mean either
    (a) fixed columns (dx1..dx10, px1..px10): wide, mostly NULL, impossible to
        query "any encounter with code X" without OR-ing 10 columns, and it
        breaks when an encounter has 11 codes; or
    (b) changing the grain to dx x px rows, which inflates the fact and
        double-counts revenue and encounters (see Decision 1).
- Bridges keep the fact clean for Q1, Q3 and Q4, which never touch them, while
  still supporting diagnosis or procedure analysis (Q2, "encounters with
  diabetes", "average LOS for heart failure").

Design details that make the bridges faster than the OLTP junction tables:
- Clustered PRIMARY KEY (encounter_key, sequence). All codes of one encounter
  are physically together, so joining the two bridges on encounter_key is a
  sequential, index-ordered match. In the OLTP, the junction tables are
  clustered by their own surrogate id, which forces random lookups.
- Integer keys only: diagnosis_key and procedure_key are joined to code
  dimensions AFTER the pairs are grouped, and only for the final 10 rows.
- Secondary indexes (diagnosis_key, encounter_key) and (procedure_key,
  encounter_key) support "find all encounters with code X".
- is_primary on the diagnosis bridge supports primary-diagnosis analysis.

Why also an aggregate table for Q2:
The bridges cannot remove the row explosion. Pairing every diagnosis with
every procedure of an encounter still produces 5.0M rows, so the bridge
version of Q2 is faster (36.5 s vs 136.4 s on MySQL) but not fast. Because the pair
counts only change when new encounters load, the ETL materializes
agg_diagnosis_procedure_pairs (about 1.2M rows) once per load, and the report
reads the top 10 from an index in under 1 ms. This is the standard approach:
keep the bridge as the flexible detail layer, and add a summary table for a
heavy query that runs repeatedly.
